The bias of escalation of commitment (throwing good money after bad) is something every human (myself included) has experienced.

## Real life example of escalation of commitment
The most famous case of it was Meta's Metaverse, where they spent tens of billions in investments and later found that it was going to be impossible to pull back. They continued pouring capital in the hopes that something would come out of it, but in the end, nothing did. At the end, all it did was waste yet more capital on an already failing project just because they had already overcommitted on initial investments.

## How scientists measure escalation of commitment biases
Psychologists measure it with a two-stage investment task: a subject invests, is told it failed, and then is offered more capital; most reinvest despite the loss, revealing the bias.

I asked, with the ever-growing dependency on LLM decision-making in businesses, and as LLMs are trained with data from the internet, would such LLMs produce similar overcommitment?

## Summary of a research paper finding that answers my question
Consequently, I found a paper that answered the exact same question I asked: [Barkett et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.01545) answered exactly this: across 6,500 trials, LLMs were rational when deciding alone but escalated ~99% in peer multi-agent settings and ~69% under compound pressure. The bias isn't built in; it's triggered by social context, which is exactly what worries me as models take autonomous business roles.
